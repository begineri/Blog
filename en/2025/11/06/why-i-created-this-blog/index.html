Why I Created This Blog

Wang
2025-11-06
something

The reason why I created this blog…

Why There

This platform is separate from QQ or WeChat Moments; not all of my “friends” can see it, and those who do see it may not necessarily be my friends.
Unlike platforms like Xiaohongshu, Douban, or Weibo, there are no ads here and no cluttered interface—everything on this site is fully customizable. I can freely experiment with different features and add elements I like without any restrictions.

How to Make It

This blog is built using Hexo. Following the steps on the official website, it was very easy to set up (and if I ran into any problems, AI was able to help).

About Other

Speaking of AI, when I really think about it, my daily life is now inseparable from it. Whether it’s big or small matters, I can always ask AI, and it always gives me a reasonable answer—most of the time, the answers are actually quite satisfactory.
There’s no denying that AI has helped me learn many things from scratch. When diving into a field that’s almost completely new to you, it’s inevitable to feel a bit lost at first. However, the problems you encounter in the beginning aren’t that complicated,which means AI can effectively help you solve problems and clear the hurdles you face as a beginner (for example, when setting up this blog, there were some steps I wasn’t sure how to handle—I just asked the AI, and it provided a solution tailored to my specific situation).
My reliance on AI has gradually increased without me even realizing it. I still remember when I was setting up my development environment for an introductory course last year—I kept running into one error after another, and my solution was to copy the error message into the browser and search. The process of searching for answers was often long and arduous; sifting through a mountain of information to find exactly what I needed was extremely difficult and time-consuming.Often, I’d spend an hour searching to fix one bug, only to encounter three new ones—or sometimes, I’d never even manage to resolve it (though my own search skills certainly played a part).
Faced with an environment that wouldn’t configure properly, I asked a senior teaching assistant for help. Her approach was to simply copy all my error messages—andFeed it to Deepseek and ask, "How do I fix this error?", the AI actually solved the problem.Looking back, this was the first time I realized that AI could help solve problems like this—and, taking it a step further, that AI could replace search engines. Perhaps from that moment on, I gradually started using direct searches less often, and since then, its applications have expanded beyond fixing code errors to cover all sorts of areas, such as:

Is there a way to do xxx?
Is there a better way to write this code?
Explain the derivation process for this formula.
How do I use this feature (screenshot)?
How do you indent the first line in Markdown text?

I’ve noticed that, aside from a few simple tasks (like searching for an official website), I’ve pretty much replaced search engines with AI. And experience has shown that this is actually quite efficient most of the time. Plus, AI is very good at answering follow-up questions—you just need to ask in the simplest terms, and it always gives you a reasonably satisfactory answer.In contrast, with search engines, you have to go out of your way to find the right wording to get the answer you want. For a lazy person like me, it’s only natural to embrace the former.
In the process of using AI, I’ve gone from The Person Being Asked Questions to The person who asked the question. You may have endless questions, and AI always provides an answer to your endless questions. As for whether you’re satisfied, that’s up to you to decide. Even if you’re not satisfied, you can keep asking questions until you get tired or bored.
I often forget that AI-generated answers are merely the result of a series of mathematical calculations—just a string of probabilistic numbers—and lack real-world “meaning.” They’re nothing like the language and answers that people generate from Consciousness, and there’s no way to guarantee their accuracy.But the more I use it, the more amazed I am by what it’s capable of. It’s just a pile of data, yet it can provide answers that meet such a wide range of needs across so many different scenarios.(As for how AI handles logic, it actually relies on high probabilities to make “logical” inferences; when it comes to absolute truth or falsehood, LLMs must rely on external tools for verification, such as a “calculator” or a “code interpreter”—for more details, see this recently published article). This often prompts me to remind myself not to rely on it too heavily—but then again, most of the time, the answers AI provides are indeed spot on X
The last time I realized I couldn’t live without AI was a while into this semester, when I noticed that every single day—and I mean every single day, not “almost every day”—I’d open Doubao and bombard it with questions. Most of the time, I’d take photos of slides from class and ask it to explain them, or have it help me with my homework problems.Then, perhaps as the coursework gradually got more difficult, Doubao could no longer answer those questions, so I started turning to Gemini—and by recently, I realized I couldn’t live without it. From small things like homework solutions to bigger projects like setting up this blog, optimizing code, or building a CPU, I’ve been happily chatting with it about everything.
I’ve been wondering if this might make me increasingly lazy and unwilling to engage in deep thinking on my own. So, at times, I’ve started to resist the urge to ask the AI directly, instead taking a moment to think it through myself and being a little more patient—and I’ve indeed found that in many cases, I can figure things out on my own, and the answers I come up with are actually better than those provided by the AI.
AI has further increased the density of information I receive. I can send it 1,000 characters and say, “Summarize it into 200 characters,” and it will do just that. I can then read this condensed text (though readers have no way of knowing whether it accurately conveys the original meaning). The information density here is very high,While such text may seem to save time and effort in the short term, I believe this high-intensity reading does indeed cause people to tire more quickly, and it’s hard to say whether it actually improves efficiency.
But it’s true—AI can be like a teacher who’s always there to answer your questions, which is a huge help for self-learners.You can delve deeply into a single topic and figure out everything you want to know. Around this time last year, I was studying linear algebra. I couldn’t find answers to the problems anywhere online, and when I asked the AI at the time, it couldn’t provide a coherent, correct answer either. So whenever I encountered a problem I really didn’t understand, I had no choice but to set it aside. In the end, I only learned the material in a half-baked way,I didn’t understand many of the concepts; I just crammed by practicing past exam questions right before the final. After the exam, those concepts faded from my memory, and even now, when I need to apply that linear algebra knowledge, it still feels so vague that I can’t put it into practice. Looking back, I think if I’d had access to today’s AI back then, my performance in linear algebra wouldn’t have been so poor.
This got me thinking: Will students’ widespread use of AI reduce the need for teachers? When students encounter problems, they’ll turn directly to AI for help, followed by classmates and teaching assistants, and only then to the teacher? In the past, teachers would sometimes help students solve their most perplexing problems, but now their role has become much simpler:they just need to show a PowerPoint presentation in class and then leave—students don’t even need to listen to them read from the slides anymore. Instead, they can simply take a screenshot of the parts they don’t understand and send it to AI, which can solve most of their problems—this is far more efficient than sitting through a lecture.This outperforms 85 percent of teachers; the remaining 10–20 percent stand out, teaching courses where students actually pay attention.These teachers don’t just limit themselves to reading the text on a PowerPoint slide; they have a truly unique understanding of the material they’re teaching, and they genuinely want to impart their knowledge to students. Their course content isn’t cookie-cutter or mediocre—it’s not something just anyone can replace—and this is what sets them apart from AI and ultimately keeps their students engaged.As for the remaining teachers who have already been replaced by AI, I can only say goodbye to them (and their classes).
